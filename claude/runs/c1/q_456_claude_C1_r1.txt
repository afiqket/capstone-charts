Based on the United States data from the chart's CSV (1956–2023):

## General Trend: Inverse Relationship

**Annual working hours — declining**
- 1956: **~2,023 hours** per worker
- Fell fairly steadily through the 1960s–70s to **~1,850 hours** by the mid-1970s
- Continued a gentler decline, reaching a low of **~1,765 hours** in 2009 (Great Recession)
- Since then roughly flat with a slight rebound, ending at **~1,789 hours** in 2023
- **Net change: down ~235 hours (~12%)**

**GDP per capita — rising strongly**
- 1956: **~$20,300** (international-$, 2021 prices)
- 2023: **~$72,900**
- **Net change: up ~3.6× (~259%)**, a steady long-term climb interrupted only by brief dips during recessions (1974–75, early 1980s, 1991, 2008–09, and the 2020 COVID downturn)

## Summary
Over this period the U.S. shows a clear **inverse trend**: workers put in **fewer hours** each year while **GDP per capita grew substantially**. In other words, Americans became far more prosperous while working less — productivity gains allowed rising output per person alongside a shrinking work year. Most of the decline in hours occurred before ~1975, after which working hours stabilized around 1,750–1,850 even as GDP per capita kept climbing.
