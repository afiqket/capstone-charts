Yes — generally, more doses tracked with a lower death rate. This is clearest in the age‑standardized **"All ages"** series that the chart displays by default (the note explains it's age‑adjusted precisely so vaccination‑rate differences between old and young don't distort the comparison).

The pattern is most visible during the high‑mortality wave of Feb–Mar 2022, where the four series spread out in a clean dose‑gradient (deaths per 100,000):

| Week | 0 or 1 dose | 2 doses | 3 doses | 4 doses |
|------|------------|---------|---------|---------|
| 2022‑02‑27 | 11.76 | 11.04 | 3.32 | 0.83 |
| 2022‑03‑06 | 11.58 | 9.94 | 3.95 | 0.68 |
| 2022‑03‑13 | 10.42 | 8.40 | 3.58 | 0.39 |

So at the peak, the unvaccinated/minimally‑vaccinated group had ~10–14× the death rate of the 4‑dose group, with 2 and 3 doses falling in between — a consistent "more doses, fewer deaths" ordering.

A few caveats worth noting:

- **It's a near‑monotonic gradient, not a perfect one.** "0 or 1 dose" and "2 doses" are often close together (and 2 doses occasionally edges higher), but 3 doses and 4 doses are reliably lower during the deadly periods.
- **The gap collapses in low‑mortality periods.** From mid‑2022 onward, when overall death rates fell to ~1 per 100,000, the four lines converge and even cross, so the dose ordering becomes noisy and inconsistent. The protective separation is mainly a feature of the high‑transmission waves.
- **These are death *rates* (deaths ÷ group size), not raw counts**, and the comparison is observational — so it reflects association, not a controlled measurement of vaccine effect.

Bottom line: yes, higher‑dose groups generally had lower COVID death rates, with the effect strongest exactly when it mattered most (the mortality peaks).
